### 1.1 Training

What I recall, with the confidence I would put on each:

- **The form.** The Catalan *comanda* is the Crown of Aragon's version of the western Mediterranean contract in which an investor (*comanador*) entrusts money or goods to a party (*comanatari*) who travels and trades with them, typically by sea, and returns capital plus a share of profit. My recollection is of the formula `recognosco me habere et tenere in comanda a te` (the agent acknowledging receipt), a destination clause, the phrase *ad usum (et consuetudinem) maris*, often a peril formula of the type *ad risicum et fortunam Dei, maris et gentium*, a profit clause, and the notarial obligation of the agent's goods. I recall a bilateral variant in which the agent also contributes capital and profit and loss are divided, called *comanda-societat* or *societas*. These recollections are generic; I cannot tie any of them to a dated Barcelona act.
- **Agent's share.** By analogy with Genoa and Marseille I expect a quarter of the profit to the agent in the unilateral form and a half in the bilateral, with other fractions occurring. I do not recall a Catalan study that measures the distribution. This expectation is borrowed from the Italian and Provençal material, which is exactly the kind of transfer the blind pass is meant to test.
- **Loss.** My prior is that in the unilateral form the investor bore loss of capital from the peril of sea and men and the agent lost his labour, and that the agent bore loss caused by his own fault or departure from instructions. I am less sure whether Barcelona acts *state* this or leave it to custom (*usus maris*).
- **Relation to the Italian commenda.** General historiographical position as I recall it: the comanda is the Catalan member of the commenda family, with the usual origins debate (Roman, Byzantine, Jewish or Islamic *qirāḍ* antecedents). Works I recall on that debate: A. L. Udovitch, *Partnership and Profit in Medieval Islam* (1970) and his Speculum article on the origins of the western commenda (1962); J. H. Pryor, "The Origins of the Commenda Contract", *Speculum* 52 (1977), and his *Business Contracts of Medieval Provence* (1981), on the Marseille notary Amalric (1248). R. S. Lopez & I. W. Raymond, *Medieval Trade in the Mediterranean World* (1955), translates sample contracts. R. Harris, *Going the Distance* (2020), treats the commenda's migration. I recall these as general positions, not as findings about Barcelona.
- **Catalan historiography.** C. Carrère, *Barcelone, centre économique à l'époque des difficultés, 1380–1462* (1967), and M. Del Treppo, *I mercanti catalani e l'espansione della Corona d'Aragona nel secolo XV* (1972), both use comandas, and I recall Del Treppo stressing their wide social spread of investors in the fifteenth century. A. García Sanz & M. T. Ferrer i Mallol, *Assegurances i canvis marítims medievals a Barcelona* (1983), is a companion edition on insurance and exchange. I have a vague sense that Vic preserves some of the oldest notarial registers (from the 1230s), which fits the period on the type row, but I recall no specific study of Vic comandas: nothing specific.
- **Normative texts.** I recall that the *Llibre del Consolat de Mar* (the *Costums de la mar*) has chapters on the comanda: that the agent must account, must not deviate from the agreed voyage, and is answerable for loss caused by deviation. I also believe the *Costums de Tortosa* (1270s) regulate *comanda* and *societat*. I do not recall chapter numbers or wording. None of this is in the bundle, and none of it can carry a cell in this pass.

## 2. The edition as a whole, before you read it

1. **Composition per 100 documents.** About 72 contracts placing capital or goods with a party who trades with them (comandas proper, with a handful of deposits or loans styled *comanda*); 10 returns, releases or quittances (*apoca*, *definitio*, *absolutio*); 4 accounts or liquidations; 4 cessions, renewals or procurations to collect a comanda; 5 partnerships or loss-sharing comandas; 5 other (protests, arbitrations, inventories, testamentary clauses mentioning comandas). An edition titled *Comandas* and selected by its editors should be dominated by the contract itself, with the other acts included because they show the contract in operation.
2. **Uniformity.** No. I expect thirteenth-century acts to be short Latin recognitions with a destination and a profit share, often goods rather than coin; fourteenth-century acts to carry fuller peril and obligation formulas and more varied profit fractions; fifteenth-century acts to include Catalan, longer instructions, more money comandas, comandas for land trade or for a time rather than one voyage, and some that read as loans or deposits with the capital guaranteed. I expect loss-sharing acts to be concentrated before c. 1350.
3. **Peril of sea and men.** About 50 of every 100 contracts, with the share rising through the fourteenth century as formulas lengthen. I am unsure about the fifteenth: a Catalan formula (*a ventura de mar e de gents*) may replace the Latin one, or guaranteed comandas may lower the share.

## 3. How you will assign an act to a form

1. **Assignment.** To `comanda`: an act in which the investor alone supplies the capital and the act states a loss rule that does not divide the capital loss between the parties. In practice that means a peril formula placing the capital at the investor's risk (*ad risicum et fortunam tuam*, *ad fortunam Dei, maris et gentium* with the risk stated as the investor's), or, the minority case, a clause placing the whole capital loss on the agent (*salvum in terra*, *sine periculo tuo*). The latter stays in `comanda` because the loss is not shared; it becomes evidence against CF2 = 0. To `comanda_ad_societatem`: an act stating that loss is shared between the parties, whether pro rata to contributions or in fixed shares (*ad lucrum et dampnum commune*, *pro rata*, *de lucro et perdita*), or one in which both parties contribute capital and the loss clause divides it. **The phrase *ad usum (et consuetudinem) maris* on its own, without a clause saying whose risk the capital is at, I will treat as a reference to custom and not as a stated loss rule.** Such an act goes to neither row, with the census note "loss by custom only". This is the decision I am least sure of and the one with the largest consequence for the counts, so I will report every count both ways: with these acts in neither row, and with them added to `comanda` as a sensitivity check. The coded cells will rest on the first count only.
2. **Unstated or ambiguous loss terms.** An act with no loss terms goes to neither row, and I record why. An act that fits both rows (for instance, peril at the investor's risk for the capital but a loss-sharing clause for the agent's own contribution) I record as `ambiguous` in the census, assign to neither, and describe in its note. An act that fits neither (a deposit, a loan at fixed return styled *comanda*) goes to neither row as `non-comanda`, and is coded in full under the non-contract sample if I did not class it as a contract placing capital with a trading party.
3. **Non-contract acts as evidence.** Only where the act identifies the comanda it concerns and states the term it bears on. A release or quittance can carry LR3 (a profit actually divided), TS2 (the arrangement closed on return), RB1 and RB4 (a loss recorded as borne by the investor and the agent discharged), and VF1 and VF2 (how that loss was established). An account can carry CI5 (the comanda traded with other goods or apart) and LR3. A cession of the investor's rights can carry CI3, and if it shows the claim passing by mere delivery, CI4. A renewal can carry TS2 and TS3. A procuration to collect can carry CI3 at most. A dispute or arbitration can carry LP3, VF1 and VF2. A value resting on non-contract acts will say so in its note.
