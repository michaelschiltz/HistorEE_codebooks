## Exposure, disclosed before any source is opened

### A. General knowledge (training), stated so that it can be separated from what the sources say

- **Commenda (unilateral; Genoese *accomendatio*, Venetian unilateral *collegantia*).** A sedentary investor (*commendator*, *stans*) supplies all the capital; a travelling party (*tractator*) supplies labour and carries the goods or cash on one voyage and back. Profit is conventionally split three-quarters to the investor and one-quarter to the traveller. Loss of capital falls on the investor; the traveller loses his labour. The contract is voyage-bound and liquidates on return with an accounting. The investor's exposure is limited to what he entrusted; the standard textbook claim is that the commenda gave the *investor* limited liability. The traveller contracts with third parties in his own name, so third parties have recourse against him personally; I know this as a general claim, not from a page.
- **Societas maris (bilateral commenda; Venetian bilateral *collegantia*).** Investor two-thirds of the capital, traveller one-third plus labour; profit split half-and-half; loss borne pro rata to capital contributed. Also voyage-bound. I also hold the general impression that in Venetian usage *collegantia* covered both the unilateral and the bilateral contract, which sits uneasily with the type row's gloss "Venetian collegantia" as a name for the bilateral form alone: a scope question I expect the sources to raise.
- **Risk clauses.** The standard formulae put the capital at the investor's risk *ad risicum maris et gentium* / "at the risk of God, sea and people"; loss arising from the traveller's breach of instructions or fault falls on the traveller. Venetian practice required the traveller to render account and return capital and share within a fixed period after return; Venetian state courts and officials enforced that. I do not know how loss was proved in these contracts beyond a general sense that the traveller had to account and that fault shifted loss to him.
- **Origins debate.** Udovitch (1962) argues for an Islamic (*qirāḍ*/*muḍāraba*) origin or at least priority over Byzantine (*chreokoinōnia*) and Jewish (*ʿisqa*) analogues; Pryor (1977) argues for Roman-law and Byzantine roots and against a straightforward Islamic transmission. Harris has worked the commenda into a Eurasian migration story (*Going the Distance*, 2020, and earlier drafts).
- **Venice.** González de Lara's work (the 2008 *JEH* article and earlier working papers) argues that the Venetian state's enforcement and administered convoy system made the *colleganza* work across large social distance. Puga and Trefler (2014) use the *colleganza* as a channel of social mobility closed off by the *Serrata*. I know nothing specific of Merelo-Guervós and Molinari (2025) or of Held (2025).
- **Genoa.** Van Doosselaere (2009) analyses the Genoese notarial cartularies (Giovanni Scriba onward, 1154 into the 14th century) as a network of commercial contracts, and tracks the relative weight of commenda, *societas* and other contracts over time and across social groups.
- **What I believe the true answers are on the entity and transfer columns**, from general knowledge only: no juridical personhood, no property in its own name, no capacity to sue as an entity (LP1–LP3 = 0); no entity shielding in any recognised sense; no transferable or depersonalised interest in the ordinary contract; capital in effect locked in for the voyage. I expect the six works to be largely *silent* on these, and silence is `.NR`, not my general knowledge.
- **Harris's allocation / spreading / pooling triad** (cited in the vocabulary as Harris 2023) places the sea loan and, as I recall, the commenda among allocation devices. I do not remember whether he classes the bilateral contract separately.
